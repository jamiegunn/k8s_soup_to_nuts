## Readability contract (inherited from the Autoscaling Playbook, applies to every page)

Target reader: a delivery-team developer whose service has survived a few deploys and who has just received the message "your namespace is blocking our node patching," or who found 502s in the error budget on a night nobody deployed. They have never written a PDB, cannot run `kubectl drain`, and may not be allowed to list nodes.

- Every page's first section explains its topic in plain language before any YAML. The overview explains disruption itself from zero ("someone or something decides your pod has to die; the only question that matters is whether they *asked first*").
- Every Kubernetes term gets a one-sentence plain definition on first use per page (eviction, cordon, drain, PDB, budget, healthy, expectedPods, taint, preemption…), then a link to the deep page. Links supplement, never substitute, the sentence.
- No unexplained jargon, no "obviously/simply." Every formula gets a worked example with the cast's real numbers immediately after.
- **Complete-commands contract:** every command in full — real cast names, explicit `-n payments`, no elided flags, no `...`, no aliases. One plain sentence of *what it does and why you're running it* before; a `console` block of representative output after, with the line(s) to look at called out. `kubectl get pdb` is read column by column the first time it appears in the section; `kubectl get pdb -o yaml` status is read field by field the first time it appears.
- **The seat marker (new, section-specific):** drains cross the tenant/platform boundary, so every command block's first line is a comment naming the seat it runs from — `# seat: tenant` (namespace RBAC suffices), `# seat: tenant — needs create on pods/eviction` (usually granted with `edit`; the ask is in the platform-contract page), `# seat: cluster-read — nodes/PVs; ask if denied`, or `# seat: platform — shown so you can read THEIR output, you won't run it`. A reader must never discover mid-incident that a command on this site needs a role they don't have.
- **The why, always:** no knob or recommendation without its reason attached. "Set `maxUnavailable: 1`" is always "…*because* at your HPA floor of 2 it still permits one eviction and at your ceiling of 16 it still leaves 15 pods carrying the peak."
- **Tradeoffs are stated, not implied:** every meaningful choice (`minAvailable` vs `maxUnavailable`, integer vs percentage, `IfHealthyBudget` vs `AlwaysAllow`, soft vs hard spread, big G vs small G on drains, one-at-a-time vs parallel for quorum apps) carries "what you gain / what you pay" — two-column mini-table for big decisions, a "The trade:" sentence for small ones.
- **Define → observe → decide, for every measurement:** `disruptionsAllowed`, `currentHealthy`/`desiredHealthy`, the `DisruptionTarget` reason, exit codes 0/137/143 on drains, replacement time R — each gets a plain definition, the exact command/PromQL to fetch it, and an "if you see X → it means Y → do Z" rule at first load-bearing use.
- **One sequence, never re-explained (new):** the moment a page reaches "and then the pod receives its deletionTimestamp," it links to [Graceful Shutdown](/workloads/graceful-shutdown/) and stops. The section's subject is everything *before* that moment (who decides, whether they ask) and everything *after* the pod is gone (where the replacement lands, who's watching). Mechanics of the death itself belong to the existing page. Review test: grep each new page for "SIGTERM," "preStop," "grace period" — every hit must be a link or a one-clause reminder, never a paragraph.

## The citizenship contract, disruption edition (runs through the whole section)

The autoscaling section's contract is "requests are reservations on a shared pool." This section's contract is its mirror image, stated once in the overview and enforced everywhere:

**A PodDisruptionBudget is a message to the platform team, and every disruption you forbid is a maintenance window someone else absorbs.** The cluster's kernels get patched, kubelets get upgraded, nodes get rebalanced — by people who need to move your pods to do it. A budget that permits zero evictions doesn't protect you; it converts routine maintenance into a stalled job, a human with cluster-admin, and an override at a time you didn't choose (the blog post is the proof). So:

- **Always permit at least one eviction at steady state** — the `disruptionsAllowed ≥ 1` invariant, checked by command on every page that touches a PDB and by CI in the governance page.
- **Say what you can absorb, in numbers derived from your SLO**, rather than what you'd prefer (nothing, ever). The SLO thread below is how the number is derived.
- **Never make them override you.** `maxUnavailable: 0` / `minAvailable: 100%` / `minAvailable` = `replicas` require a written platform-team sign-off (the blog's rule, adopted section-wide), and the sign-off names who gets paged when the drain stalls.
- **Don't hoard the schedulable pool during drains either:** required anti-affinity, `DoNotSchedule` spread, and PriorityClass self-promotion all claim node headroom other tenants' replacements need in the same window — the landing page's good-citizen asides.
- The recurring `:::tip[Good citizen]` aside device appears wherever a knob could be set selfishly: `minAvailable`, `maxUnavailable: 0`, an oversized `terminationGracePeriodSeconds` (drains wait out G per pod — link the dual inequality on graceful-shutdown), required anti-affinity, blanket `tolerationSeconds`, `priorityClassName`.

## The SLO thread, disruption edition (runs through the whole section)

Every PDB number in this section is traceable to a promise about users — or honestly labeled as a provisional proxy — via the same ladder the autoscaling section taught (`slos-for-scaling`), with one extra rung:

```
user promise (SLO)  →  load at the moment the drain arrives  →  pods needed to hold it  →  how many may be missing  →  PDB shape + number
```

The rung that's new: **drains don't consult your load profile.** They arrive at 3 a.m. at the HPA floor *and* at 12:30 at the ceiling, so the budget must hold at *both* ends of the load table. That's why the shape matters more than the number:

| Workload (cast) | SLO (from [the canonical table](/autoscaling/slos-for-scaling/)) | At the floor | At the ceiling | PDB that holds at both | Why not the alternative |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `payments-api` | 99.9% < 800 ms / 28 d; 60 rps/pod; floor 2 (40 rps), ceiling 16 (900 rps) | 1 pod carries 40 rps ✔ | 15 pods carry 900 rps ✔ (exactly — degraded-but-inside-budget) | `maxUnavailable: 1` | `minAvailable: 2` permits **0** at the floor (the 3 a.m. jam); `maxUnavailable: 10%` = ceil(1.6) = **2** at the ceiling → 14 × 60 = 840 < 900 → SLO burn during a lunchtime drain |
| `dispatch-worker` | 99% processed < 5 min; floor 1, ceiling 8 | 0 consumers for the replacement time R (~60–90 s JVM start) — inside a 5-minute freshness budget, *stated* | 7 of 8 keep draining ✔ | `maxUnavailable: 1` | `minAvailable: 1` permits 0 at the floor — the drain waits forever for a consumer that has nowhere to go |
| `notify-worker` | 99% sent < 15 min; floor 1, ceiling 6 | same shape, bigger freshness budget | 5 of 6 ✔ | `maxUnavailable: 1` | — |
| `catalog-web` | 99% < 1 s; floor 2, ceiling 12 | 1 pod at the trough ✔ (verify per-pod capacity from web-worker page) | 11 of 12 ✔ | `maxUnavailable: 1` | — |
| `catalog-indexer` | fresh < 10 min | 0 for R, stated | — | `maxUnavailable: 1` | — |
| `valkey-primary`/`valkey-replica` (StatefulSets) | cache hit-rate/latency; failover time | one failover's write blip (Sentinel/announce time, from the shared-VIP build) | — | `maxUnavailable: 1` **per role**, separate PDBs (never one selector over both) | `minAvailable: 1` across both roles permits evicting primary and replica together |

This table lives once (on the PDB page) and its numbers are cited everywhere else — the rest-api-oracle page's 60 rps/pod, load-profile's 40/220/900/1,400 state table, slos-for-scaling's 800 ms — so the reader watches an SLO become a PDB shape the way they watched it become an HPA target.

- **The fallback ladder applies unchanged:** (a) SLO-derived; (b) proxy from observed behavior ("N−1 pods held last Tuesday's peak, PROVISIONAL"); (c) floor — "we can lose one pod, we think" written down as an objective with a TODO. Every PDB in the section (Helm values, review checklist) requires the level to be *stated* in a `# derivation:` comment, exactly like `maxReplicas`.
- **Freshness-shaped SLOs permit a zero.** Consumers at `minReplicas: 1` will be at zero for R during a drain; that is acceptable *if R < the freshness budget* and the team says so out loud. Latency-shaped SLOs never permit a zero.
- **Quorum-shaped promises** ("the cluster stays writable") permit exactly one member down at a time and nothing else — the stateful page's rule.

## The organizing frame: who decides · how it dies · where it lands

The section is structured around three questions every disruption answers, stated in the overview and used as the spine of the cheat-sheet:

1. **Who decides, and do they ask?** — the eviction gate. Four sources of death: *you* (deploy, `kubectl delete`, `rollout restart`), *the autoscaler* (HPA/KEDA scale-in), *the platform* (drain, upgrade, descheduler, VPA), *the machine* (node shutdown, pressure, taint, crash, preemption). Only the platform's tools go through the Eviction API, so only they read your PDB. Everything else you either control yourself or cannot prevent — and the section says which, per source, in one table (the **ask / wait / come back** matrix: does it consult the PDB? does it honor your grace period, and whose number caps it? who recreates the pod, and does the replacement have somewhere to go?).
2. **How does it die?** — owned entirely by [Graceful Shutdown](/workloads/graceful-shutdown/). The section links and stops (the one-sequence rule).
3. **Where does it land?** — the scheduling half nobody plans: the replacement must fit on the nodes that are *left*, under your own anti-affinity/spread/volume/quota constraints, while other tenants' replacements compete for the same headroom.

Every page states which question it serves. The "who killed my pod" decoder (the `DisruptionTarget` reason table) is the frame's observability artifact — written once on the anatomy page, cited from every other page and the error index.

### 5. pod-disruption-budgets.md — PodDisruptionBudgets, All the Way Down
Serves question 1's negotiation. Framing: "A PDB is one number and one shape. Define→observe→decide rows for `disruptionsAllowed` (0 on a healthy day → your shape permits nothing → fix the shape; 0 with `currentHealthy < desiredHealthy` → you're degraded → fix the pods, not the PDB) and `expectedPods` (0 → selector drift after a Helm rename → the PDB guards nothing). → **`unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy`** — state the rule first, plainly: under the default `IfHealthyBudget`, a Running-but-not-Ready pod may be evicted *only while the budget is currently met* (`currentHealthy ≥ desiredHealthy`); the moment you're short of healthy pods, **nothing** can be evicted — not even the broken ones. Then narrate the jam with cast numbers, and get the arithmetic right on the page: `catalog-web` at 4 replicas, `maxUnavailable: 1` → `desiredHealthy 3`. A bad config rollout leaves **two** pods in `CrashLoopBackOff` → `currentHealthy 2 < 3` → `disruptionsAllowed 0` and, under `IfHealthyBudget`, the drain of the node hosting a *broken* pod stalls on a pod that serves nothing. The jam needs more unhealthy pods than the budget spares — which is exactly what a bad rollout or a memory-pressure night produces.) `AlwaysAllow` → running-but-not-Ready pods are always evictable; the healthy ones stay protected because the budget is still unmet for *them*. Lab 10's monitoring stack is optional for one step (metrics) — the lab says so and degrades gracefully. **The cluster is one node (`lima-k3s`) and this lab uses that as a feature:** you can't drain to another node, so every mechanism is felt at its sharpest — the budget blocks, the replacement has nowhere to land, the drain blocks itself. Say this in the framing, and say what changes with real nodes (→ where-pods-land).

**"What you'll have at the end":** an `orders-api` chart whose PDB is `maxUnavailable: 1` + `AlwaysAllow` with a derivation comment; a self-eviction you ran through the Eviction API under fortio load with a clean report; a budget you made say **no** (429) and then yes; the crashloop jam reproduced and unjammed with one `kubectl patch`; a blocked `kubectl drain` you watched retry from the platform's seat, on your own cluster; a `DisruptionTarget` condition you read off a dying pod; a Job that survived an eviction without spending a retry; and the maintenance-window runbook run once against a cluster you own.

**Where things live:** PDB template changes → **into the orders-api chart** (production pattern). `eviction.json`, `budget-demo.yaml` (the readiness-file Deployment), `evictable-job.yaml`, optional `pdb-alert.yaml` → standalone in `~/k8s-labs/`.

**Steps:**
1. **Preflight** (`bash` + `console`): VMs up; `kubectl get deploy orders-api` shows `2/2`; `kubectl get pdb` shows the Lab 8 budget (`MIN AVAILABLE 1`, `ALLOWED DISRUPTIONS 1`); `kubectl auth can-i create pods/eviction` → `yes` (you're admin here — the lab notes what the production ask looks like, → platform-contract). This doubles as the quick-start gate run for real.
2. **Read the budget like the controller does:** `kubectl get pdb orders-api -o yaml` — the status block field by field (the section's first `-o yaml` read is on the PDB page; the lab repeats it against real numbers: `expectedPods: 2`, `desiredHealthy: 1`, `currentHealthy: 2`, `disruptionsAllowed: 1`, `conditions: DisruptionAllowed/SufficientPods`).
3. **The self-eviction drill, under load:** start Lab 8's loadgen Job; write `eviction.json`; `kubectl create --raw /api/v1/namespaces/labs/pods/<pod>/eviction -f eviction.json`; in a second terminal `kubectl get pdb -w` (watch `ALLOWED` go 1→0→1) and `kubectl get pods -w`; read the fortio report: clean, *because of Lab 8's preStop and grace* — "an eviction is a deletion with a bouncer in front; the bouncer decides *whether*, Lab 8 decided *how*." Catch the `DisruptionTarget` condition on the Terminating pod during its 40 s grace window (`kubectl get pod <pod> -o jsonpath='{.status.conditions[?(@.type=="DisruptionTarget")]}'`) → `reason: EvictionByEvictionAPI`. Contrast: `kubectl delete pod <other>` → no condition — "that's the difference between asking and not."
4. **Make the budget say no:** `helm upgrade orders charts/orders-api --reuse-values --set pdb.minAvailable=2` → `ALLOWED DISRUPTIONS 0` on a healthy day; evict → `Error from server (TooManyRequests): Cannot evict pod as it would violate the pod's disruption budget. The disruption budget orders-api needs 2 healthy pods and has 2 currently` — "this is what the platform team saw for six hours in the Field Note." `:::note` on percentage rounding: `--set pdb.minAvailable=80%` → still 0 (ceil(1.6) = 2). Fix the *shape*: chart edit — `values.yaml` gains `pdb.maxUnavailable`, `templates/pdb.yaml` renders one shape or the other (`{{- if .Values.pdb.maxUnavailable }}` … `{{- else }}` …), plus `unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy: {{ .Values.pdb.unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy | default "AlwaysAllow" }}` and a `# derivation:` comment in values; upgrade; `ALLOWED 1`; evict → 201. Optional beat: `--set autoscaling.enabled=true` from Lab 10 and note `expectedPods` follows the HPA's count — the 3 a.m. lesson in one `kubectl get pdb -w`.
5. **The crashloop jam, deterministic:** `budget-demo.yaml` — Deployment `budget-demo`, 2 replicas, image `busybox` (already on the node), `command: ["sh","-c","touch /tmp/ready; sleep infinity"]`, readiness `exec: cat /tmp/ready` (`periodSeconds: 2`), plus a PDB `minAvailable: 2` with `IfHealthyBudget` (explicit). Both Ready → `ALLOWED 0` (the permits-nothing trap, again, on purpose). `kubectl exec budget-demo-<a> -- rm /tmp/ready` → NotReady within seconds → `currentHealthy 1 < desiredHealthy 2` → evict the *broken* pod → **429** ("the platform can't even remove the pod that's already down"). `kubectl patch pdb budget-demo --type merge -p '{"spec":{"unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy":"AlwaysAllow"}}'` → evict the broken pod → 201; evict the healthy one → still 429 (the budget still protects it). The whole `unhealthyPodEvictionPolicy` argument in four commands.
6. **Cordon — the landing half, on one node:** `kubectl cordon lima-k3s` → `kubectl get nodes` shows `Ready,SchedulingDisabled`; `kubectl describe node lima-k3s | grep Taints` shows `node.kubernetes.io/unschedulable:NoSchedule`; `kubectl rollout restart deploy/orders-api` → the surge pod sits `Pending` with `1 node(s) were unschedulable` (`kubectl describe pod`); `kubectl rollout undo` or `uncordon` → schedules. "Cordon is *no new pods*; drain is *and evict the old ones*."
7. **A blocked drain, from the platform's seat:** `kubectl drain lima-k3s --pod-selector app.kubernetes.io/name=orders-api --ignore-daemonsets --delete-emptydir-data` (drain the *only* node, but only your pods — flags explained; implementer confirms whether `--ignore-daemonsets`/`--delete-emptydir-data` are needed on this k3s and shows the exact refusal text if omitted) → `node/lima-k3s cordoned` → `evicting pod labs/orders-api-…` ×2 → first `pod/… evicted`; its replacement `Pending` (cordoned) → `currentHealthy 1`, `desiredHealthy 1`, `ALLOWED 0` → second eviction: `error when evicting pods/"…" -n "labs" (will retry after 5s): Cannot evict pod as it would violate the pod's disruption budget.` — retrying forever (`--timeout` 0). Terminal B: `kubectl get pdb -w` + `kubectl get pods -w` showing the stalemate — *the disruptions.md three-node example in miniature: the budget is waiting for a replacement that has nowhere to land, and the drain is what removed the room.* Ctrl-C; `kubectl uncordon lima-k3s` → replacement schedules → Ready → `ALLOWED 1`. `:::note` on what a real cluster changes (the replacement lands on another node and the second eviction proceeds — *if* the other nodes have headroom; → where-pods-land) and what the platform's tool would have done at its timeout (→ platform-contract).
8. **Jobs — stop burning retries:** `evictable-job.yaml` — Job `evictable`, `backoffLimit: 0`, container `busybox sleep 300`, `podFailurePolicy: rules: [{action: Ignore, onPodConditions: [{type: DisruptionTarget}]}]`; evict its pod → pod `Failed` with `DisruptionTarget`, Job creates a fresh pod, `.status.failed` stays 0. Repeat with the policy removed → Job `Failed` (`BackoffLimitExceeded`). Console for both.
9. **Metrics and an alert** (optional — "if Lab 10's monitoring stack is still installed"): `kube_poddisruptionbudget_status_pod_disruptions_allowed{namespace="labs"}` in Prometheus; a `PrometheusRule` `PDBPermitsNothing` (`== 0 for 2m` — lab pacing, prod 30 m); trigger it with step 4's `minAvailable=2`, watch it fire, fix, watch it resolve. **Implementer must confirm kube-state-metrics is enabled in the Lab 10 trimmed values (Lab 6's full stack has it; Lab 10's trim may not) and add the `--set kubeStateMetrics.enabled=true` line if not.**
10. **The window runbook, run once:** the platform-contract before/during/after checklist against this cluster — before (PDB ≥ 1, no rollout in progress, spread n/a on one node), during (the three-terminal watch during a self-eviction), after (`kubectl get pods -o jsonpath` non-zero-exit scan → all 0 thanks to Lab 8).
11. **Teardown:** delete `budget-demo`, the Job, `eviction.json`; keep the chart's new PDB shape (state what survives for from-lab-to-prod); `kubectl uncordon` if anything is still cordoned; remove the PrometheusRule if created.
12. **Recap + links out:** overview, pdb §PDB×HPA, where-pods-land, platform-contract, graceful-shutdown ("Lab 8 made each death clean; Lab 11 decided which deaths are allowed and when; production adds the nodes the replacements land on and the people who run the drain"). Lab 11 is the terminal step of the learning-paths "Survive maintenance" journey.
