THE MODEL

Kubernetes asks three questions

Strip it to its job: a control loop that makes the cluster match what you asked for. To run one workload it needs exactly three things — and there is no fourth.

Why it is a loop

The request you set at Door 1 is the number the autoscaler divides by at Door 3, and Door 2 decides whether either number is believable. Get Door 1 wrong and Door 3 is wrong by the same factor.

DOOR 1 · COST

One asymmetry decides everything

A CPU limit costs you latency. A memory limit costs you the process.

CPU is compressible: exceed your share and the kernel makes you wait. Memory is not — there is no ‘wait’, so the only move the kernel has is to kill. That single difference is why the two knobs are set in completely different ways.

Memory: request = limit

Pin them together. You are then killed at exactly the number you reserved, with no surprising gap in between — and the scheduler has actually set that much aside for you.

CPU: set a request, think hard about a limit

A quota throttles precisely the tail you are judged on. Plenty of teams deliberately run a CPU request with no CPU limit, and watch the throttle ratio instead.

The kill is not a Java exception

OOMKilled, exit 137, is the kernel. Your OutOfMemoryError handling never runs, no stack trace is printed, and nothing in your code could have caught it.

The JVM twist

Your heap is only the biggest tenant of the container. Metaspace, thread stacks, the code cache and direct buffers live there too — which is exactly how a pod dies at its memory limit while every heap dump looks healthy.

DOOR 2 · TRUTH

Three probes, three different questions

startupProbe

“Have you finished booting?”

Buys a slow JVM all the time it needs, without making the liveness probe permanently lenient.

readinessProbe

“Should I send you traffic right now?”

The only probe that gates the Service. Failing it removes you from the load balancer — it does not restart you.

livenessProbe

“Are you wedged, so only a restart helps?”

It must judge nothing but itself. If it can fail for a reason a restart cannot fix, it is wired wrong.

The classic outage

A readiness probe that checks a downstream dependency. The dependency blips for thirty seconds → every replica reports not-ready → every replica leaves the load balancer at once → a wobble becomes a 100% outage. Readiness answers for you, never for your dependencies.

And leaving is half of truth. When Kubernetes wants a pod gone, it removes the endpoint and sends SIGTERM at the same moment — these race. preStop: sleep 5 lets endpoint removal propagate first; your shutdown hook then finishes in-flight work inside the grace period.

DOOR 3 · RESPONSE

Scale on the thing that actually runs out

The autoscaler's arithmetic is usage ÷ request. It divides by the number you set at Door 1 — so a request nobody can defend produces scaling that is confidently wrong.

CPU is the default, not the answer

Measured on a service that spends its time waiting on a database: across the point where latency broke its promise, CPU utilisation moved from 0.61 to 0.66. Busy threads moved from 0.58 to 0.94. An autoscaler watching CPU would never have fired.

Pick the number that saturates when the promise breaks — and was already moving one step earlier.

The ceiling usually isn’t yours

16 replicas × a connection pool of 10 = 160 database sessions. Past the budget the database refuses new connections — for everyone sharing it, not just for you. Your scale-out becomes the batch team's outage.

Before raising a replica ceiling, do the multiplication and find out whose number you are spending.

Re-check it

Which number saturates first is a property of what a request does. Any release that adds a call to something slow can move it — so the signal is re-measured after the release, not inherited from last quarter.

WHEN NUMBERS FIGHT

Both numbers are correct

kubectl top says the pod is using 40% of its CPU. The p99 has tripled. Someone says “we have headroom — it must be GC.” A week goes into GC flags, the database, and the network.

The answer was a third number: the container was throttled in 41% of its 100-millisecond scheduling periods while averaging 31% of its limit. Bursty work meeting a quota. An average hides a wall you are hitting ten times a second.

Neither instrument lied. They answer different questions, because they stand in different places — and there are only three places to stand.

1

Outside the process

The kernel's accounting for your container: CPU charged, periods throttled, bytes resident, kills delivered. Exact about what it cost; blind to what it was doing.

2

Inside, continuously

Your app measuring itself: requests by route, pool waits, heap by pool, GC pauses. Knows what it was doing — as an average, and as a sample every few seconds.

3

Inside, on demand

Stop it and ask: a thread dump, a heap histogram, a flight recording. Sees everything about one process right now, and only because you asked.

The rule

When two numbers disagree, don’t stare harder at one — change instrument. A number that makes no sense is usually a correct number read from the wrong place.

WHEN IT BREAKS

Five things Kubernetes does to your pod

Admit it, schedule it, start it, route to it, kill it — always in that order. Each step writes down why in exactly one place, and it is rarely the place you were looking.

Three faults, one word

CrashLoopBackOff means three different things. Exit 1 is your code — logs --previous has its last words. 137 with OOMKilled is the kernel at your memory limit. 137 beside a liveness event is Kubernetes deciding you were wedged. And an empty log is not evidence about your app: it means the container never started.

THIS WEEK

Four things to do with your own service

1

Open your values file and read it as three doors

For every number in it, can you say where it came from? A request nobody can defend is the number your autoscaler is dividing by.

2

Check your readiness probe touches nothing but you

Five minutes and one line. It is the most common way a thirty-second blip somewhere else becomes your full outage.

3

Read kubectl get pods as the five steps

STATUS covers admit, schedule and start; READY is route; RESTARTS is kill — and a young AGE during an old incident means the evidence already died with the last pod.

4

Find both views of one of your pods

Your app's own metrics endpoint, and the cluster's view of the same container. Know which number comes from where before the day you need to tell them apart.
